# UI vs UX
After reading the article, I realized that even though I understood that UI and UX are two different concepts, I didn’t fully grasp where that difference lies. UI, or User Interface, is everything you see and touch on a website, like buttons, menus, icons, and the overall design. For example, the Twenty Fifteen WordPress theme has a super clean UI, with buttons you instantly understand what they’re for. You could say UI is about making things clear and easy to use.

UX, on the other hand, is more about how you feel while using the site. It’s not just whether the buttons work, but whether you like being there, if it frustrates you, or if you’d return to the site. The article talks about how UX aims to make users happy and connect with the site. For example, the Amax WordPress theme isn’t just pretty—it flows so well that it’s a joy to navigate, whether on your phone or computer. But if the UX fails, like with the HealthCare.Gov site the article mentions, where everything was a mess and nothing loaded, people leave in less than two seconds. UX is like the overall vibe of a place—if you don’t feel comfortable, you don’t come back.

Even though they’re two different things, UI and UX need each other. An incredible interface is useless if the experience is chaotic, or vice versa. I started thinking about how, for example, the WordPress admin panel can have a functional UI, but if it’s not well thought out for the user, managing a site becomes a headache. This reflection opened my eyes to how important it is to understand the people who will use what we design. It’s not just about making it work, but about making them smile while they use it.

Something similar happens in our project, where we collect information about the processes involved in gathering notes and filling out documents in a file. No matter how great of a User Interface we design, if the therapists don’t feel comfortable using it, it won’t be much use. For example, being able to change a patient’s status is great, but if we didn’t have immediate visual feedback after performing an action, therapists wouldn’t know if the change went through correctly and would have to check it, adding complexity to the process.

Another example is when a secretary checks a file. She has a UI that lets her access the documents she’s allowed to see, but if she had to open each document to check its existence, it would take forever, and she doesn’t even have access to all of them. That’s why we use a checklist of the existing documents so she doesn’t have to go through each one individually.
